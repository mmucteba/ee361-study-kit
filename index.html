Introduction · Lesson 1

Why power engineering, and why the grid is AC

Before any formula: what this course is about, what a power system looks like, and the one physical fact that forced the whole world onto high-voltage AC.

After this lesson you can

  • Name the energy conversions EE361 studies and where each device sits in the grid.
  • Explain, with a formula, why transmission lines run at hundreds of kV.
  • Explain why that requires AC (transformers) and what DC lacked historically.

1.1What EE361 is about

Electromechanical energy conversion means turning electrical energy into mechanical energy and back. A motor converts electrical to mechanical (your phone's vibration motor, a 30 MW ship propulsion motor). A generator converts mechanical to electrical (a 7.5 MW wind turbine, the 700 MW units at Itaipu with a 16 m rotor turning at 91 rpm). A transformer is not an electromechanical device, nothing moves, but it is the reason the grid works. Power electronics does static conversion with switches.

Course order: Chapter I gives you the circuit tools for power (this page). Chapter II magnetic circuits, III transformers, then machines and power electronics. Every later chapter uses Chapter I's phasors, complex power and three-phase relations, so this is the foundation.

1.2The shape of a power system

1.3Why high voltage: the I²R argument

A line has resistance \(R\). The current that flows through it heats it at a rate

\[ P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R . \]

For a fixed power to deliver, \(P = V I\) (DC, or AC at unity power factor), so \(I = P/V\). Substitute:

\[ P_{\text{loss}} = \left(\frac{P}{V}\right)^{2} R = \frac{P^{2}R}{V^{2}} . \]
The key result. Loss falls with the square of voltage. Raise the voltage 10 times, the current drops 10 times, and the heating drops 100 times. Same wire, same delivered power.

The alternative, a thicker wire with smaller \(R\), costs copper or aluminium on every kilometre. Raising voltage is far cheaper.

1.4Why that forces AC

Edison's early grids were DC. LN1 lists their problems:

  • Transformers do not work with DC. They need a changing magnetic flux (Faraday's law, Chapter II).
  • So the DC voltage could not be raised or lowered (power electronics did not exist yet).
  • So transmission had to happen at low voltage, which by the formula above is very inefficient. DC plants had to sit within about a mile of their users.

AC with transformers fixes all three: step up for transmission, thinner and cheaper cables, long distances, and better overall efficiency even after paying the transformers' own losses. Today HVDC exists for special long links, made possible by power electronics, but the grid you use is AC.

That is why the rest of this chapter is about AC circuits: sinusoids, phasors, complex power, and three-phase systems.

Check yourself

A line delivers the same power at 154 kV instead of 34.5 kV. Roughly how does its I²R loss change?

Loss scales as \(1/V^2\). The voltage ratio is \(154/34.5 = 4.46\), so the loss ratio is \(4.46^2 \approx 19.9\). Current falls 4.5 times, loss falls about 20 times.

Key takeaways

  • Motors and generators convert energy; transformers change voltage level; together they make the grid.
  • \(P_{\text{loss}} = P^2R/V^2\): high voltage is the cheapest way to cut transmission loss.
  • Transformers need AC, so the grid is AC.

DC systems · Lesson 2

Power and energy

The two words people mix up most. Power is how fast energy moves. Energy is how much moved. Bills charge energy, cables and machines are rated by power.

After this lesson you can

  • Compute energy from a power profile, including time-varying loads.
  • Convert between J, Wh and kWh, and compare energy across forms (food, lifting, batteries).
  • Write the power balance of a source feeding a load through a cable.

2.1Power is a rate, energy is an amount

Voltage is energy per charge (J/C). Current is charge per second (C/s). Their product is energy per second:

\[ p(t) = v(t)\,i(t)\quad[\text{W} = \text{J/s}] \]

Energy is power accumulated over time, the area under the power curve:

\[ W = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} p(t)\,dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,i(t)\,dt \quad [\text{J} = \text{W·s}] \]

For constant power this is just \(W = P\,\Delta t\). For a piecewise-constant profile, add rectangles.

Unit you will use most
\[ 1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6\ \text{MJ} \]

2.3Source, cable, load: the power balance

KVL and power conservation give the two working equations of the chapter, here in their simplest DC form:

\[ V_s = V_L + I R_{\text{line}}, \qquad P_s = P_L + I^2 R_{\text{line}} . \]

Efficiency of delivery is \(\eta = P_L/P_s\). For AC we will keep exactly these ideas but with phasors and complex power (Lessons 4–6).

Check yourself

A motor runs at 2 kW for 15 minutes, then 0.5 kW for 45 minutes. What is its average power over the hour?

Energy \(= 2\times0.25 + 0.5\times0.75 = 0.5 + 0.375 = 0.875\ \text{kWh}\). Divide by 1 h: \(0.875\ \text{kW}\). Average power must be time-weighted. The last option has the right number but the wrong unit: kWh is energy, not power.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing kW (power) with kWh (energy). If power is in kW, time must be in hours to get kWh.
  • Doubling a cable resistance that is already given as the loop value.
  • Calling a motor's rated 4 kW the cable loss. The rating is the load; the loss is \(I^2R\) in the cable.

Key takeaways

  • \(W = \int p\,dt\): energy is the area under power.
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.
  • \(P_s = P_L + I^2R\) and \(V_s = V_L + IR\) for a series cable.

Single-phase AC · Lesson 3

Sinusoids and RMS values

AC voltage swings positive and negative 50 times a second, so its average is zero. Yet it heats your kettle. RMS is the number that tells you how much.

After this lesson you can

  • Read amplitude, angular frequency, period and phase from \(v(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t+\theta)\).
  • Derive \(V_{\text{rms}} = V_m/\sqrt2\) and explain what RMS physically means.
  • Say which waveform leads or lags and convert a phase angle into a time delay.

3.1Anatomy of a sinusoid

\[ v(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \theta_v) \]
  • \(V_m\): peak (maximum) value, volts.
  • \(\omega = 2\pi f\): angular frequency, rad/s. Turkey and Europe: \(f = 50\) Hz, so \(\omega = 314.16\) rad/s.
  • \(T = 1/f\): period. At 50 Hz, \(T = 20\) ms.
  • \(\theta_v\): phase angle, where the waveform is in its cycle at \(t=0\).

Sen writes sine instead of cosine. It does not matter, as long as you use one convention for every quantity in a problem.

3.2RMS: the heating-equivalent value

Put a sinusoidal current through a resistor. The instantaneous heating is \(p = i^2R\), which is never negative: heat is produced on both half-cycles. Ask: which DC current \(I\) would produce the same average heat?

\[ I^2 R = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T i^2(t)\,R\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T i^2(t)\,dt} \]

The name says the recipe: Root of the Mean of the Square. For a sinusoid, \(\cos^2\) averages to exactly \(1/2\) over a cycle (because \(\cos^2 x = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos 2x\)), so

\[ I_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\tfrac12 I_m^2} = \frac{I_m}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.707\,I_m . \]

3.3Phase: who comes first

Two sinusoids of the same frequency differ only by amplitude and phase. With voltage as reference,

\[ v = \sqrt2\,V\cos\omega t, \qquad i = \sqrt2\,I\cos(\omega t - \varphi) . \]

If \(\varphi>0\) the current reaches each peak later: the current lags. Inductive loads (motors) do this. If \(\varphi<0\) it leads, like capacitors. The time shift is

\[ \Delta t = \frac{\varphi}{\omega} = \frac{\varphi^{\circ}}{360^{\circ}}\,T . \]

Example: 30° lag at 50 Hz is \(\tfrac{30}{360}\times 20\ \text{ms} = 1.67\) ms. You can see this live in the next lesson's rotating phasor.

Worked exampleRead everything off two waveforms
\(v(t) = 200\sqrt2\cos(100\pi t)\) V and \(i(t) = 10\sqrt2\cos(100\pi t - \pi/6)\) A. Find f, T, peaks, RMS values, the phase relation and the heat in a 0.4 Ω cable carrying i.
  1. \(\omega = 100\pi\) rad/s, so \(f = \omega/2\pi = \) 50 Hz, \(T = \) 20 ms.
  2. Peaks: \(V_m = 200\sqrt2 = \) 282.8 V, \(I_m = 10\sqrt2 = \) 14.14 A. RMS: divide by √2, 200 V and 10 A.

    Questions write "200 V" meaning this RMS value.

  3. Phase: \(\varphi = \pi/6 = 30^\circ\), positive, so the current lags by 30° = 1.67 ms.
  4. Cable heat: \(I_{\text{rms}}^2R = 10^2\times0.4 = \) 40 W average. (Peak instantaneous heat is \(I_m^2R = 80\) W, not what anyone asks for.)
Check yourself

A 50 Hz current lags its voltage by 45°. By how much time does the current peak come after the voltage peak?

\(\Delta t = (45/360)\times 20\ \text{ms} = 2.5\ \text{ms}\). A quarter period (90°) would be 5 ms.

Common mistakes

  • Using peak current in \(I^2R\). It doubles the loss.
  • Confusing √2 (peak ↔ RMS) with √3 (line ↔ phase, Lesson 8).
  • Deciding lag or lead from the sign of one angle alone. Compare the two waveforms' angles.

Key takeaways

  • RMS = the DC value with the same heating. For a sinusoid, \(V_{\text{rms}} = V_m/\sqrt2\).
  • Lagging current peaks later: inductive. Leading: capacitive.
  • \(\Delta t = (\varphi/360^\circ)\,T\).

Single-phase AC · Lesson 4

Phasor representation and phasor diagrams

Solving circuits with cosines and derivatives is painful. A phasor turns each sinusoid into one complex number, and calculus into algebra.

After this lesson you can

  • Turn a sinusoid into an RMS phasor and back.
  • Move between rectangular and polar form, and know which form to use for which operation.
  • Use impedance \(Z = R + jX\) and \(V = ZI\), and read lag or lead from the sign of X.

4.1A sinusoid is the shadow of a rotating arrow

Picture an arrow of length \(\sqrt2 V\) rotating counter-clockwise at \(\omega\) rad/s. Its projection on one axis traces exactly \(\sqrt2V\cos(\omega t+\theta)\). Every quantity in a single-frequency circuit rotates at the same speed, so the rotation carries no information. Freeze the picture at \(t=0\) and keep only length and angle. That frozen arrow is the phasor:

\[ v(t) = \sqrt2\,V\cos(\omega t + \theta) \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf{V} = V\angle\theta . \]

In power engineering the phasor length is the RMS value (Sen eq. B.3–B.4). The frequency is known separately.

ExploreRotating phasors and the waveforms they draw
What to notice: both arrows rotate together, so the angle between them never changes. That fixed angle is all a phasor diagram needs. When the blue current arrow is behind the red voltage arrow (clockwise from it), its waveform peaks later: lagging.

4.2Complex numbers you actually need

With \(j^2 = -1\) (engineers use j, because i is current), the same phasor has two forms:

\[ a + jb \;=\; M\angle\theta, \qquad M = \sqrt{a^2+b^2},\quad \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(b,a),\quad a = M\cos\theta,\ b = M\sin\theta . \]

4.3Impedance: Ohm's law for AC

Differentiating a sinusoid rotates it by 90° and scales it by ω. That is why inductors and capacitors become imaginary numbers:

\[ Z_R = R, \qquad Z_L = j\omega L, \qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C}, \qquad \mathbf V = Z\,\mathbf I . \]

A general load is \(Z = R + jX = |Z|\angle\theta\), with \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(X/R)\) (Sen B.5–B.7). Then

\[ \mathbf I = \frac{\mathbf V}{Z} = \frac{V\angle 0}{|Z|\angle\theta} = \frac{V}{|Z|}\angle{-\theta} . \]

So the current lags the voltage by exactly the impedance angle. \(X>0\) (inductive): lags. \(X<0\) (capacitive): leads. \(X=0\): in phase. KVL, KCL, series and parallel rules all keep their usual form with complex numbers.

Check yourself

\(\mathbf V_1 = 100\angle 0^\circ\) V and \(\mathbf V_2 = 100\angle 90^\circ\) V are in series. What is the magnitude of the total?

Convert and add: \(100 + j100\), magnitude \(\sqrt{100^2+100^2} = 141.4\) V at 45°. Magnitudes only add when the phasors point the same way.

Common mistakes

  • Adding magnitudes of phasors that point in different directions.
  • Using \(\tan^{-1}(b/a)\) without checking the quadrant; use atan2 or draw it.
  • Writing \(Z_C = j\omega C\). The capacitor's reactance is negative: \(-j/(\omega C)\).

Key takeaways

  • Phasor = RMS length + angle; all quantities rotate together so only relative angles matter.
  • Add in rectangular, multiply/divide in polar.
  • \(\mathbf I = \mathbf V/Z\): current lags by the impedance angle. X > 0 lag, X < 0 lead.

Single-phase AC · Lesson 5

Complex power and power factor correction

An inductive load draws more current than its useful power needs. This lesson shows where the extra goes, how to measure it with P, Q and S, and how a capacitor gives it back.

After this lesson you can

  • Derive \(P = VI\cos\varphi\) from instantaneous power and explain reactive power physically.
  • Use \(\mathbf S = \mathbf V\mathbf I^* = P + jQ\) and the power triangle, with correct signs.
  • Size a capacitor to raise the power factor, and say what it changes and what it does not.

5.1Instantaneous power has two parts

Take \(v = \sqrt2V\cos\omega t\) and \(i = \sqrt2I\cos(\omega t-\varphi)\). Multiply and use \(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)]\):

\[ p(t) = v\,i = \underbrace{VI\cos\varphi}_{\text{constant}} \;+\; \underbrace{VI\cos(2\omega t - \varphi)}_{\text{averages to zero}} . \]

Power pulses at twice the supply frequency. Its average is the constant part, the real power:

\[ P = VI\cos\varphi \quad [\text{W}] . \]

Expand the second term further (Sen eq. B.10) and one piece, \(-VI\sin\varphi\sin2\omega t\), is pure back-and-forth: energy stored in the load's magnetic or electric field each quarter-cycle and returned the next. Its peak is the reactive power:

\[ Q = VI\sin\varphi \quad [\text{var}] . \]

Q does no net work, but the current that carries it still flows through every wire and still heats them.

5.2Complex power and the power triangle

Package P and Q into one complex number using phasors:

\[ \mathbf S = \mathbf V\,\mathbf I^* = VI\angle(\theta_v-\theta_i) = VI\cos\varphi + jVI\sin\varphi = P + jQ . \]

The conjugate is there so that inductive loads come out with \(Q>0\) (Sen B.14). The magnitude \(|\mathbf S| = VI = \sqrt{P^2+Q^2}\) is the apparent power in VA, what cables, transformers and generators must be rated for. The power factor is

\[ \text{PF} = \frac{P}{|\mathbf S|} = \cos\varphi . \]

5.3Power factor correction (single-phase)

A capacitor draws a current that leads by 90°, so it absorbs negative Q. Put it in parallel with an inductive load and the two reactive powers cancel locally: the load still gets its Q, but now from the capacitor next to it instead of from the far-away source.

\[ Q_C = P\left(\tan\varphi_{\text{old}} - \tan\varphi_{\text{new}}\right), \qquad Q_C = \omega C V^2 \;\Rightarrow\; C = \frac{Q_C}{\omega V^2} . \]

What changes: source Q, |S|, current, cable loss. What does not change: the load's P and its own Q. An ideal capacitor uses no real power.

Worked exampleCorrect a 4 kW, 0.8 lag load on 230 V, 50 Hz to 0.95 lag
Find the capacitor and the current before and after.
  1. Old Q: \(4000\tan(36.87^\circ) = 3000\) var. Target Q: \(4000\tan(\cos^{-1}0.95) = 4000\times0.3287 = 1314.7\) var.
  2. Capacitor supplies the difference: \(Q_C = 3000 - 1314.7 = \) 1685 var.
  3. \(C = Q_C/(\omega V^2) = 1685/(314.16\times230^2) = \) 101.4 µF.
  4. Current: before \(4000/(230\times0.8) = 21.7\) A; after \(4000/(230\times0.95) = \) 18.3 A. 16 % less current, 29 % less cable heat.
Check yourself

A load's PF is 0.8. Which statement is true?

\(I = P/(V\,\text{PF})\), so \(1/0.8 = 1.25\). PF is not efficiency (option 1). PF 0.8 can be leading or lagging, since cos is even (option 2). \(Q/P = \tan(\cos^{-1}0.8) = 0.75\) (option 4).

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the conjugate in \(\mathbf S = \mathbf V\mathbf I^*\). Q flips sign.
  • Treating PF as efficiency.
  • Adding |S| values of different loads. Add P and Q separately, then take the magnitude.
  • "Supplies/injects reactive power" for a load means Q < 0.

Key takeaways

  • \(\mathbf S = \mathbf V\mathbf I^* = P + jQ\), \(|S| = VI\), PF = P/|S| = cos φ.
  • Lagging (inductive) Q > 0; leading (capacitive) Q < 0.
  • A shunt capacitor supplies Q locally: same P, less current, less loss.

Single-phase AC · Lesson 6

A load at the end of a cable

Everything so far, combined: the source has to supply the load plus the cable, and its voltage has to be higher by a phasor drop. This is exactly 2025 MT1 Q1.

After this lesson you can

  • Find the cable's real and reactive power, and the energy it wastes.
  • Find the source P, Q and PF from a complex power balance.
  • Find the source voltage by phasor KVL and compute voltage regulation.

6.1The cable absorbs \(|I|^2 Z\)

The cable is a series impedance \(Z_c = R + jX\) carrying the load current. The voltage across it is \(\mathbf I Z_c\), so its complex power is

\[ \mathbf S_c = (\mathbf I Z_c)\,\mathbf I^* = |\mathbf I|^2 Z_c = |I|^2 R + j\,|I|^2 X . \]

The real part is heat. Note \(|\mathbf I|^2\) is the squared RMS magnitude, never the square of the complex phasor.

6.2Power balance and phasor KVL

\[ \mathbf S_s = \mathbf S_L + \mathbf S_c, \qquad \mathbf V_s = \mathbf V_L + \mathbf I Z_c . \]

The source PF uses source P and Q, so it differs from the load PF. Voltage regulation compares magnitudes:

Three-phase AC · Lesson 7

Balanced three-phase systems

Almost all the world's electricity is generated, transmitted and used as three phases. Three sinusoids 120° apart turn out to have some almost magical properties.

After this lesson you can

  • Write the three phase voltages of a balanced abc system in time and phasor form.
  • Show why balanced currents cancel in the neutral.
  • Show why total three-phase power is constant, and list why we use three phases.

7.1Where three phases come from

A synchronous generator has three identical stator windings placed 120° apart around the machine. A DC-excited rotor spins past them and induces three equal sinusoids, each 120° behind the previous one (Sen Fig. B.3):

\[ v_a = \sqrt2V_p\cos\omega t,\quad v_b = \sqrt2V_p\cos(\omega t - 120^\circ),\quad v_c = \sqrt2V_p\cos(\omega t + 120^\circ) \]
\[ \mathbf V_a = V_p\angle0^\circ,\qquad \mathbf V_b = V_p\angle{-120^\circ},\qquad \mathbf V_c = V_p\angle{+120^\circ} . \]

The phases peak in the order a, b, c: positive sequence. Balanced means equal magnitudes and exactly 120° spacing.

7.2Property 1: the neutral current vanishes

With identical loads, the three currents are equal in size and 120° apart. Add them as phasors:

\[ \mathbf I_n = \mathbf I_a + \mathbf I_b + \mathbf I_c = I\left(1 + \angle{-120^\circ} + \angle{120^\circ}\right) = I\left(1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + j\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\right) = 0 . \]

So the return conductor can be thin or absent. Three wires do the job of three separate single-phase circuits that would need six.

7.3Property 2: total power is constant

Each phase's power pulses at \(2\omega\) (Lesson 5). But the three pulsing parts are spaced \(2\times120^\circ = 240^\circ\) apart and cancel just like the currents:

\[ p_{3\phi}(t) = p_a + p_b + p_c = 3V_pI_p\cos\varphi \quad\text{(constant, Sen B.36).} \]

For a motor this means constant shaft torque with no supply-driven vibration. A single-phase motor sees torque pulsing 100 times a second.

7.4Why three, not two or five?

  • More power per wire. Single-phase: 2 wires carry \(VI\). Three-phase: 3 wires carry \(3VI\). Twice the power per conductor.
  • Constant power, as shown above.
  • Rotating magnetic field: three windings 120° apart with these currents make a field that spins smoothly, the basis of induction motors (EE362).
  • Three is the smallest number that gives all of this. More phases add wires and cost with little gain.

In Turkey the standard is now 230 V line-to-neutral (it used to be 220 V). Its peak is 325 V, and line-to-line it is \(230\sqrt3 = 400\) V. Why √3? Next lesson.

Check yourself

In a balanced three-phase system, what is the frequency of the ripple in the total instantaneous power?

Each phase pulses at 100 Hz, but the three pulses are 240° apart and sum to zero. Only the average \(3V_pI_p\cos\varphi\) remains.

Key takeaways

  • Balanced: equal magnitudes, 120° apart, sequence abc.
  • Balanced currents sum to zero → no neutral current.
  • Total instantaneous power is constant, \(3V_pI_p\cos\varphi\).

Three-phase AC · Lesson 8

Y and Δ connections: line and phase quantities

There are two ways to wire three windings or three loads. Each gives a different relation between what you measure on the lines and what each element actually sees.

After this lesson you can

  • Define line and phase voltage and current, for both connections.
  • Derive \(V_{LL} = \sqrt3V_{ph}\) at +30° (Y) and \(I_L = \sqrt3I_{ph}\) at −30° (Δ) from the phasor diagram.
  • Draw both phasor diagrams that exams ask for.

8.1Four quantities, two connections

8.2Y: line voltage is √3 bigger and 30° ahead

In Y each line feeds exactly one branch, so \(I_L = I_{ph}\). A line voltage spans two branches (LN2 slide 21):

\[ \mathbf V_{ab} = \mathbf V_{an} + \mathbf V_{nb} = \mathbf V_{an} - \mathbf V_{bn} = V_p\left[1 - \left(-\tfrac12 - j\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\right] = V_p\left(\tfrac32 + j\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = \sqrt3\,V_p\angle30^\circ . \]

Magnitude \(V_p\sqrt{9/4 + 3/4} = \sqrt3V_p\), angle \(\tan^{-1}\!\big((\sqrt3/2)/(3/2)\big) = 30^\circ\). The other two follow by symmetry: \(\mathbf V_{bc} = \sqrt3V_p\angle{-90^\circ}\), \(\mathbf V_{ca} = \sqrt3V_p\angle150^\circ\).

8.3Δ: line current is √3 bigger and 30° behind

In Δ each branch sits directly between two lines, so \(V_{ph} = V_{LL}\). But each line node joins two branches. KCL at node a:

\[ \mathbf I_a = \mathbf I_{ab} - \mathbf I_{ca} = I_p\angle{-\varphi}\,\big(1 - 1\angle120^\circ\big) = \sqrt3\,I_p\angle(-\varphi - 30^\circ) . \]

Same geometry as the Y voltages, mirrored: the line current is √3 longer and 30° behind its branch current (Sen B.52).

Three-phase AC · Lesson 9

Three-phase power calculations

Power formulas that work for either connection, and a trick to turn any Δ load into an equivalent Y.

After this lesson you can

  • Compute P, Q, |S| of a balanced load from phase or line quantities.
  • Convert a Δ load to its equivalent Y, \(Z_Y = Z_\Delta/3\).
  • Tell "same kVA in a new connection" apart from "same impedance in a new connection".

9.1One formula for both connections

Three identical branches, each with phase quantities \(V_p, I_p\) and angle φ:

\[ P_{3\phi} = 3V_pI_p\cos\varphi, \qquad Q_{3\phi} = 3V_pI_p\sin\varphi, \qquad |S_{3\phi}| = 3V_pI_p . \]

In Y, \(V_p = V_L/\sqrt3\) and \(I_p = I_L\). In Δ, \(V_p = V_L\) and \(I_p = I_L/\sqrt3\). Either way \(3V_pI_p = \sqrt3V_LI_L\):

\[ |S_{3\phi}| = \sqrt3\,V_{LL}\,I_L, \qquad P_{3\phi} = \sqrt3\,V_{LL}I_L\cos\varphi, \qquad Q_{3\phi} = \sqrt3\,V_{LL}I_L\sin\varphi . \]
φ is still the branch angle, the impedance angle, not the angle between \(V_{LL}\) and \(I_L\) (that one is off by 30°). The fastest current formula you will use: \(I_L = \dfrac{|S_{3\phi}|}{\sqrt3V_{LL}} = \dfrac{P}{\sqrt3V_{LL}\,\text{PF}}\).

9.2Δ to Y: \(Z_Y = Z_\Delta/3\)

Find what Y load draws the same line current. For the Δ load, using \(\mathbf V_a + \mathbf V_b + \mathbf V_c = 0\) (Sen B.59):

\[ \mathbf I_a = \mathbf I_{ab} - \mathbf I_{ca} = \frac{(\mathbf V_a - \mathbf V_b) - (\mathbf V_c - \mathbf V_a)}{Z_\Delta} = \frac{3\mathbf V_a - (\mathbf V_a + \mathbf V_b + \mathbf V_c)}{Z_\Delta} = \frac{\mathbf V_a}{Z_\Delta/3} . \]

A Y load gives \(\mathbf I_a = \mathbf V_a/Z_Y\). Equal currents require \(Z_Y = Z_\Delta/3\). This is the door to the per-phase method of the next lesson: every balanced circuit can be drawn as Y.

9.3Reconnecting the same impedances

2025 Q2 held each load at 5 kVA, so total power did not change. A different (and very common) question: take three fixed impedances and move them from Y to Δ on the same supply. Each now sees √3 more voltage, so \(V^2/Z\) gives 3 times the power.

Three-phase AC · Lesson 10

Per-phase equivalent circuit, line model and voltage regulation

A balanced three-phase network is three copies of the same circuit shifted by 120°. Solve one copy, then multiply. This is the method behind every feeder question.

After this lesson you can

  • Draw the per-phase equivalent of any balanced system (Δ sources and loads included).
  • Find line current, sending-end voltage, line losses and regulation.
  • Walk a multi-bus system with generator injections using KCL.

10.1Building the per-phase circuit

  1. Represent every source as an equivalent Y: \(\mathbf V_a = \dfrac{V_{LL}}{\sqrt3}\angle{-30^\circ}\) relative to \(\mathbf V_{ab}\) (Sen B.62). Usually just take \(V_{LL}/\sqrt3\angle0^\circ\) as reference.
  2. Represent every Δ load by its Y equivalent \(Z_\Delta/3\), or simply by its total \(P + jQ\).
  3. Keep the line impedance per phase as given (Ω/phase). No loop doubling here.
  4. Connect all neutrals with an ideal wire. It carries no current in a balanced system.

10.2The working equations

\[ \mathbf I = \left(\frac{\mathbf S_{3\phi}}{3\mathbf V_r}\right)^{*} = \frac{P - jQ}{3V_r}, \qquad \mathbf V_s = \mathbf V_r + Z_{\text{line}}\mathbf I, \qquad V_{s,LL} = \sqrt3|\mathbf V_s| \]
\[ P_{\text{loss}} = 3|I|^2R, \qquad \mathbf S_s = \mathbf S_r + 3|I|^2Z_{\text{line}}, \qquad \text{VR}\% = \frac{V_{s,LL}-V_{r,LL}}{V_{r,LL}}\times100 . \]

Q at the receiving bus is the net Q: load minus any capacitors there. That is how compensation reduces the line current.

10.3Several buses and generators

When generators inject power along the way, walk from the bus whose voltage you know:

  1. Current toward the known bus from its load: \(\mathbf I = (\mathbf S/3\mathbf V)^*\).
  2. Next voltage upstream: \(\mathbf V_{\text{next}} = \mathbf V + Z\mathbf I\).
  3. Generator at that bus injects \(\mathbf I_g = (\mathbf S_g/3\mathbf V_{\text{next}})^*\). KCL: the line further upstream carries \(\mathbf I - \mathbf I_g\).
  4. Repeat. The last generator's output is \(3\mathbf V\mathbf I^*\) at its terminals.

Sign care: a generator at leading PF absorbs reactive power, so its injected \(\mathbf S_g = P - jQ\). The official Sample Q2 in Practice is exactly this.

Three-phase AC · Lesson 11

Power factor correction in three-phase systems

Real plants have many loads, some lagging, some leading. Add them correctly, decide what compensation is needed and where, and size the actual capacitors or reactors.

After this lesson you can

  • Add parallel loads with signed reactive power and find the overall PF.
  • Compute the compensation needed and decide between capacitors and reactors.
  • Size per-branch C or L for Y and Δ banks, respecting component voltage ratings.

11.1Parallel loads add as complex powers

\[ \mathbf S_{\text{tot}} = \sum_k (P_k + jQ_k), \qquad \text{PF}_{\text{tot}} = \frac{P_{\text{tot}}}{|\mathbf S_{\text{tot}}|} . \]

Never average PFs or add |S| values. Leading loads (Q < 0) cancel lagging ones, sometimes enough that no compensation is needed at all.

11.2How much compensation

\[ Q_{\text{target}} = \pm P\tan(\cos^{-1}\text{PF}_{\text{target}}), \qquad Q_{\text{comp}} = Q_{\text{target}} - Q_{\text{old}} . \]

\(Q_{\text{comp}}<0\): add capacitors (they supply Q). \(Q_{\text{comp}}>0\): add reactors (a leading load is over-supplying Q). Compensate only to the limit; more is wasted money.

11.3Sizing the bank

A capacitor of C on branch voltage \(V_b\) supplies \(\omega CV_b^2\); a reactor absorbs \(V_b^2/(\omega L)\). The bank rating Q is the total for three branches:
